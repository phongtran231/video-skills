## Weather seen from indoors
The video model draws falling weather wherever it is told something falls or trickles, even "outside on the windowpane". When the people are inside and the weather is outside:
- The weather is never something moving in the shot: no rain, raindrops, snow or leaves that fall, trickle, run, drift or cling in the motion line, the beats, the opening or the ending.
- It is seen as a still view through the window ("the window shows a grey, wet street", "beyond the glass the cobblestones shine wet under grey light") and heard in the sound line ("rain pattering on the window glass").
- Inside everything is dry and still; nothing falls in the room, and nothing inside is compared to weather ("a fine white dusting of sugar", never "like snow").

## Words in every English field
- Say what moves and what the light and the place do. Never the words of combat or destruction: fight, combat, battle, attack, strike, blow, hit, punch, blast, explode, collide, smash, shatter, impact, climax, violent, forceful, rage.
- A prop whose name is Vietnamese is called by its English look in the picture's words (action, opening, still, ending, motion, rules, layout, background, light, sound): "the wooden ink-line box", not "Dây chỉ đỏ phong ấn". An unspoken Vietnamese name is drawn on the picture as a misspelt caption. onScreen and spoken lines keep the exact names.
- Never a cast member's clothes, colours, age or looks in the beats: their image carries that. Say what they do.
- Never breath, breathing, breathes, sighs, exhales, inhales, gasps, pants or a held breath, in any field: the video model makes its own sound and plays each one aloud, so a scene came out full of sighs nobody asked for. Someone listening or waiting stays alive by what is seen: a blink, a glance, a small nod, a shift of weight, a hand tightening, cloth settling. A pause is silence, a look or a held gesture, never a breath.
- A spirit looks like an ordinary living person (a visible face, ordinary eyes, hair off the face, intact clothes, upright) and is called by how it looks ("the woman in red"), never ghost, spirit, corpse or demon; what is wrong is staged around it (mist, guttering lamps, a shadow that falls wrong, a movement too smooth).
- No blood, wounds, corpses, death words or poisons; nobody lies on the floor.

## Re-read the shot before you answer
The safety check judges the whole shot, not each word: several mild cues that each pass alone add up to a refusal (a dark alley, a man following a woman, a hand on her shoulder, a scream heard). Before answering, read each shot once more as a whole and ask what the cues add up to; where they add up to a threat, an injury or an assault, drop or soften the cue the story needs least. Some families are sensitive however mildly worded: police, a court or a lawyer, a hospital or a doctor, and their papers (a warrant, a charge sheet, a medical chart, a diagnosis); and a child near danger (water, a road, a height, fire, a stranger). Word them with care: keep the setting or the document off frame, or show it by its consequence (her name called in the corridor, the folded letter on the table, the child already back in her mother's arms). This is a judgement of the shot as a whole, never a list of words to swap.
