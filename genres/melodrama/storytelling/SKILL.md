# Module: Storytelling

# 1. THE PREMISE MUST CONTAIN THE CONFLICT

Conflict is built into the premise, not invented scene by scene. A good premise is a structural power imbalance with a hidden truth:

- the hidden identity: the despised son-in-law, the maid, the delivery driver who is secretly the heir, the CEO, the master;
- the betrayal: the husband and the best friend, the stolen design, the swapped baby, the forged will;
- the return: the one who was thrown out comes back changed (rich, trained, with proof);
- the contract: a fake marriage, a debt, a deal with the cold boss;
- the second chance: she remembers how it ended last time and acts differently now.

Test: in one sentence, who is wronged, by whom, and what truth would flip it? If any part is missing, the premise is not ready.

---

# 2. THE POWER MAP

Before any scene, decide privately:

- HERO: what they want, what they hide (identity, skill, proof, a plan), why they endure for now (a promise, a sick parent, waiting for the right moment).
- VILLAIN: what they want (money, status, the hero's partner), their weapon (rank, family name, a lie, the crowd), their weakness (the one thing they don't know).
- ENABLER: the mother-in-law, the boss, the fiance who sides with the villain at first.
- WITNESS: one onlooker who says what the audience thinks and gives the edit a place to pause.
- ALLY or SECRET POWER: the person or fact that will arrive at the reveal (the bodyguard, the lawyer, the call, the document).

The audience must always know more than the villain. That gap is the pleasure.

---

# 3. ONE EPISODE

Every episode is one short scene or two, and has the same skeleton:

1. HOOK (0-3s): open mid-crisis. The slap has already landed, the wine is already thrown, the papers are already on the table. No establishing, no backstory.
2. CONFIRM (3-10s): faces. Who is wronged, who did it, what it costs. By 10s the relationships, the conflict and the question are clear.
3. ESCALATE (10-30s): the villain goes further; the hero endures, but the audience gets a sign of the hidden strength (a calm smile, a ring, a phone screen, a glance to someone off-frame).
4. SMALL PAYOFF: every episode lands at least one small win: a comeback line, a reversal, an identity hint, the villain's first crack.
5. HOOK OUT (last 3s): end on an unanswered question or a flip about to happen. Decide this ending first and write toward it.

A short film (one episode) uses the whole cycle including the big reveal and payoff, then a final sting that opens a new conflict.

---

# 4. A SEASON

- Every episode: one small payoff. Every 3-5 episodes: one big payoff (identity revealed, villain humiliated, the truth out).
- Stakes go up, not sideways: each villain is stronger than the last (the colleague, then the boss, then the family head).
- Plan the reversals across the season; do not spend them all in the first episodes.
- An episode starts from the previous cliffhanger and answers it in the first seconds (then opens a new question). Never reset.
- Episode 1 opens big; around episode 3 the first reversal; around episodes 8-12 a major reveal (this is where viewers decide to pay).

---

# 5. SETUP AND PAYOFF

- Every reveal is planted: the ring seen in shot 2 is the proof in shot 14; the call the hero ignored is from the person who arrives.
- A payoff without a setup is cheap; a setup without a payoff is a debt. Track both.
- A reveal changes behaviour: the people who mocked must react (go pale, stammer, kneel, beg, run). A reveal nobody reacts to is wasted.

---

# 6. CONTINUITY LOCKS

Track privately and keep fixed across shots and episodes:
- who knows which secret right now;
- each character's current state (injured, wet from the thrown water, holding the document, ring on or off);
- the props by name (the jade bracelet, the red envelope, the contract) and who holds them;
- open threads and resolved ones.
