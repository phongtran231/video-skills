# Module: Storytelling

# THREE KINDS OF EPISODE (choose one per film, vary them across the series)
1. CARING: an animal looking after an old person (or a sick child, a tired parent) through one day or one evening: cooking a simple meal standing on a stool at the stove, feeding them with a spoon, bringing their glasses, tucking a blanket, walking them in the garden, sitting with them as they fall asleep. A small obstacle in the middle (the porridge burns, the medicine bottle won't open, rain on the walk) solved with the animal's own nature (a dog's nose finds the lost glasses). It ends on a tender beat: the old person's hand on the animal's head, a "thank you, my boy", the animal asleep at their feet.
2. SAVING: the animal notices something wrong before anyone (a smell of smoke, a fall in the garden, a child too near the pond, a storm coming) and acts: runs, barks, fetches a neighbour, pulls the blanket, presses the phone's button with a paw, guides people to the spot. Keep the danger mild and shown by its signs and the animal's urgency (smoke curling under a door, a cane lying on the path, an empty swing), never an injury or a person in pain on screen; the turn is the animal's courage, the end is the relief and the hug.
3. BICKERING: two or three animals in the same house or yard trolling each other: a cat and a dog over the sofa, the last sausage, the warm sunny spot, the owner's attention; a parrot who repeats the wrong thing; a duck who always wins. Setup, escalation in three beats (each trick topping the last), a reversal where the schemer gets caught by their own trick, and a final gag or a grudging truce.

# SHAPE OF A FILM
- Hook (first 2-3 seconds): the most surprising image of the episode: a dog in an apron flipping a pancake, a cat in reading glasses reading the newspaper to grandpa, two cats glaring across a sausage.
- Setup: who lives here and what today is.
- The middle: the caring tasks, the danger noticed and the rush to help, or the escalation of tricks.
- Turn and payoff as above; a last small beat of animal nature as a button (the dog chases his tail after the hug; the cat knocks the trophy off the shelf).

# THE CAST
Animals with clear species and one look each (a golden retriever in a red apron, a grey tabby in round glasses, a red fox in a green scarf), and the humans they live with (a grandmother, a grandfather, a child) in the same 3D style. Two or three characters per film is best; at most four.
