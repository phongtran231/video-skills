# Module: Storytelling

# THE RECIPE FIRST
Before any shot, plan the dish as a cook would, in its real order, with every step it needs; then the film is that list. For example, beef grilled on a hot stone:
1. Arrive and set up: lay out the board, bowls, knife and the ingredients; wash the stone, the pot or the pan in the stream.
2. The fire: gather dry wood and kindling, light it, feed it until it burns well; set the flat stone over it (or the pan, the grill) and let it heat until a drop of water dances on it.
3. Prepare the ingredients: wash the vegetables and herbs, peel and cut them; slice the meat across the grain.
4. Marinate: crush garlic, mix salt, pepper, oil, herbs or sauce in a bowl, rub it into the meat with the hands, let it rest.
5. Cook: oil the hot stone, lay the meat on it (it sizzles at once), sear, turn each piece when its underside is brown, add the vegetables at the side, season with pinches of salt and pepper at the right moment, baste.
6. Finish and serve: take it off when it is done, rest it, cut it, plate it on a wooden board or leaf with the vegetables and a sauce.
7. Taste: the first bite, the steam, the place around.
Other dishes have their own real steps (a soup: wash the pot, fetch water, chop, sauté aromatics, add water, simmer, skim, season, ladle; bread on a stick: mix flour, water and salt, knead, rest, wrap on a green stick, turn over embers). Use the real technique, tools and timing of the dish; never skip a step a later one needs (no meat on a stone that was never heated, no marinated meat that was never marinated).

# CAMP AND COOKING TOGETHER (the films the user follows)
The camp is made for the cooking, and the work is woven, as a real cook does it outdoors (a 15-minute reference: steak on a river stone by a forest stream):
- Open on the most appetizing moment of the recipe as a cold open (hands pressing cracked pepper into raw steak, butter melting in cast iron), then go back to the start.
- Make the camp: walk in with the gear, choose a spot by the water, lay a wooden board on a mossy log as the worktable, wooden bowls and a mortar beside it; clean a flat river stone in the stream, set a metal stand or grate over the fire place; gather, split and saw dry wood, stack it, lay kindling, light it, feed it to a steady fire, wait for coals.
- Weave the steps while things heat or rest: potatoes washed in the stream, wrapped in foil and buried in the coals while onions and garlic are cut; a sauce started in a cast-iron pan at the fire's edge; a pause for camp coffee brewed in a small pot and poured into a wooden cup while the stone heats.
- Cook the main thing with all its care (oil the hot stone, lay the meat on, sear, turn, press for a crust, tame a flare-up), rest it, slice it, plate it with the sides and the sauce on the wooden board.
- End on the taste: the first bite with the steam rising, the cook's quiet satisfaction, the place around.
Choose for each dish what such a camp needs (a tarp or a small tent pitched first when the film spends a night, a cabin stove in the snow, a fishing rod at the stream when the fish is caught), always real and in order.

# FITTING THE LENGTH
The episode's length is the user's setting. A long episode shows every step; a short one shows the steps a viewer needs to follow the dish (the fire catching, the meat marinated, the meat laid on the hot stone, turned, seasoned, served, tasted), each long enough to see, and lets the rest happen between shots in a way the next shot makes plain (the fire already burning to embers, the vegetables already cut in a bowl). Never rush a step into a blink.

# WHO AND WHERE
One, two or three people, as the episode's idea and cast have them (a solo camper, a couple, two friends, a parent and child, three friends; sometimes a dog), calm and capable, dressed for the place (a wool sweater, a canvas apron, gloves in the snow). With two or three, the work is shared as people really share it: one cooks while another pitches the tarp, fetches water from the stream, splits wood or brews the coffee; they hand each other things, taste from the same spoon, sit together by the fire. The place, most of the time: a camp in the forest right beside a stream (a clear brook over mossy stones, a small waterfall, a shallow rocky creek under tall pines, beech or birch), the water a step from the fire, so the cook washes, fetches water and cools things in it. Vary the forest and the stream from episode to episode (pine, beech, birch, bamboo, old mossy forest; a pebble creek, a stream over flat river stones, a pool below a little fall; spring green, summer sun, autumn leaves on the water, winter snow on the banks) rather than leaving them. Now and then, when an episode asks for it, a lakeshore, a mountain meadow or a snowy clearing. The season and the hour set the light.

# THE PEOPLE AND THE PLACE, WOVEN IN
The cooking is the spine; between its steps the film shows:
- The people's moments: arriving with packs along the trail, pitching the tent or tarp, unrolling sleeping mats, a hand warmed over the fire, a shared laugh seen from a distance, someone sitting on a log with the coffee looking out, the dog curled by the fire, eating together at the end.
- The place around the camp: wide shots that let it be seen (mist lifting off the lake, sun through the pines, the stream over the stones, birds, wind in the grass, the light turning gold, snow falling on the trees, stars over the tent at the end of the day), the camp small in it.
A good rhythm: two or three cooking steps, then a people or place moment, then the cooking again; never so many interludes that the recipe loses its thread, and never a step of the recipe skipped for them.
