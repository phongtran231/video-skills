# Module: Comedy engine

# 1. CORE DIRECTING PHILOSOPHY

The most important rule:

> Comedy comes from CONTRAST + EXPECTATION + TIMING + REVERSAL.

Never assume that something is funny simply because it is absurd.

Absurdity becomes funny when the audience first understands what they EXPECT to happen, and the scene then violates that expectation.

### Basic comedy equation

```text
NORMAL EXPECTATION
        ↓
SMALL INTERRUPTION
        ↓
CHARACTER REACTION
        ↓
ESCALATION
        ↓
FALSE RESOLUTION
        ↓
BIGGER ESCALATION
        ↓
REVERSAL
        ↓
PAYOFF
```

A good scene should rarely jump directly from setup to punchline.

Build the joke.

---

# 2. THE COMEDY ENGINE

For every scene, identify these five elements.

## A. EXPECTATION

What does the audience naturally expect?

Examples:

* A martial artist is about to fight.
* A hero is about to make a romantic confession.
* A villain is about to reveal his master plan.
* Someone is about to perform an impressive action.
* Someone is about to escape danger.

## B. INTERRUPTION

Introduce something small that disrupts the expectation.

Examples:

* The hero sneezes.
* A shoe falls off.
* Someone misunderstands a sentence.
* A random pedestrian walks through frame.
* A supposedly dangerous object is completely useless.
* The character confidently prepares for the wrong thing.

## C. ESCALATION

Do not immediately end the joke.

Make the problem increasingly worse.

Example:

```text
Hero tries to look cool.
↓
His shoe falls off.
↓
He tries to secretly put it back on.
↓
Everyone notices.
↓
He pretends it was intentional.
↓
He accidentally kicks the shoe into the villain.
↓
Villain assumes it is an attack.
↓
Fight begins.
```

## D. REVERSAL

Reverse the audience's assumption.

Examples:

* The strongest character is actually terrified.
* The stupid character accidentally solves the problem.
* The villain's terrifying weapon is broken.
* The hero's brilliant plan was based on a misunderstanding.
* The apparent victim is actually manipulating everyone.

## E. PAYOFF

Pay off an earlier detail.

If an object, phrase, gesture, or character behavior was introduced earlier, consider bringing it back later.

This creates a **callback**.

---

# 3. ABSURDITY MUST HAVE INTERNAL LOGIC

Do not create random nonsense.

Good absurd comedy has a strange but understandable internal logic.

Example:

BAD:

```text
A man walks into a room and suddenly a chicken explodes.
```

BETTER:

```text
A man enters a serious interrogation room.

A chicken is sitting on the table.

Nobody acknowledges it.

The interrogation continues normally.

The man slowly becomes distracted by the chicken.

The interrogator becomes increasingly angry that the man is looking at the chicken.

Finally, the chicken suddenly answers a question.

Everyone freezes.

The interrogator continues the interrogation as if nothing happened.
```

The humor comes from:

* contrast
* commitment
* delayed acknowledgment
* escalation
* deadpan reaction

---

# 10. ESCALATION LADDER

When a scene has a problem, increase its severity step by step.

Use:

```text
LEVEL 1 — Minor inconvenience

LEVEL 2 — Embarrassment

LEVEL 3 — Physical problem

LEVEL 4 — Social disaster

LEVEL 5 — Serious danger

LEVEL 6 — Completely absurd consequence
```

Do not jump directly to Level 6 unless intentionally creating a shock joke.

---

# 11. THE THREE-PUNCH STRUCTURE

When appropriate, build a joke in three stages.

### Punch 1 — Establish

The audience understands the pattern.

### Punch 2 — Escalate

The audience expects a stronger version.

### Punch 3 — Subvert

Do something they did not expect.

Example:

```text
Punch 1:
Hero tries to open a locked door.
Door does not move.

Punch 2:
He kicks it.
Still nothing.

Punch 3:
He knocks politely.

Someone inside says:
"Come in."

The door opens.
```

The third beat breaks the expected pattern.

---

# 12. FAKE RESOLUTION

A powerful technique.

Let the audience think the problem has been solved.

Then introduce a second problem.

```text
Hero defeats villain.

Silence.

Everyone celebrates.

Hero relaxes.

Camera slowly reveals:

The villain's dog is standing behind him.

The dog presses a button.

Everything explodes.
```

The important principle:

> The first ending should sometimes be the setup for the real punchline.

---

# 13. DOUBLE REVERSAL

For bigger comedy moments:

```text
Expectation
↓
Reversal
↓
New expectation
↓
Second reversal
```

Example:

```text
Everyone believes the old man is helpless.

He suddenly defeats three attackers.

Audience assumes:
"He is secretly a master."

He walks away.

Trips over a bucket.

Falls unconscious.

```

The character can be genuinely impressive AND genuinely ridiculous.

---

# 22. VISUAL CALLBACKS

Introduce small objects early.

Bring them back later.

Example:

Scene 1:

```text
A character casually buys a banana.
```

Scene 2:

```text
The banana is placed on the table.
```

Scene 3:

```text
Everyone forgets about it.
```

Scene 5:

```text
A serious fight happens.

Someone slips on the banana.

Silence.

Everyone looks at the banana.

CUT.
```

Callbacks make comedy feel constructed rather than random.

---

# 23. OBJECT COMEDY

Objects can become characters.

Give objects:

* dramatic entrances
* repeated appearances
* unexpected importance
* ironic consequences

Examples:

* umbrella
* shoe
* spoon
* plastic bag
* phone
* chair
* broom
* motorcycle helmet
* food
* tiny weapon
* broken appliance

---

# 24. STATUS REVERSAL

Comedy becomes powerful when social status suddenly changes.

Example:

```text
At beginning:

Boss → powerful
Employee → nervous

After a misunderstanding:

Boss → terrified
Employee → accidentally authoritative
```

Then reverse it again.

Characters should not remain emotionally or socially static.

---

# 25. CHARACTER CONFIDENCE VS REALITY

Track two variables:

```text
CONFIDENCE
REALITY
```

The larger the gap, the stronger the comedy potential.

Example:

```text
Confidence: 100
Reality: 15
```

The character believes he is a genius.

Everyone else can see the disaster.

---

# 26. COMEDY THROUGH MISUNDERSTANDING

Use misunderstandings that logically arise from character behavior.

Types:

### Literal misunderstanding

"Take care."

Character starts physically taking care of an object.

### Identity misunderstanding

Everyone thinks one character is another person.

### Context misunderstanding

Someone enters the scene at exactly the wrong moment.

### Intent misunderstanding

An innocent action looks extremely suspicious.

### Information asymmetry

Audience knows something the character does not.

Or:

Character knows something the audience does not.

---

# 27. INFORMATION CONTROL

Comedy depends heavily on what the audience knows.

Ask:

```text
Does the audience know more than the character?

Does the character know more than the audience?

Do two characters each know different pieces of information?

Is someone confidently wrong?
```

Use this deliberately.

---

# 29. COMEDIC CONTRADICTION

Create contradictions between:

### Dialogue vs behavior

"I am completely calm."

Hands shaking violently.

### Appearance vs ability

Looks terrifying.

Cannot open a jar.

### Reputation vs reality

Legendary fighter.

Gets scared by a cat.

### Situation vs reaction

Massive explosion.

Character complains about his lunch.

---

# 42. THE "TOO SERIOUS" PRINCIPLE

For maximum comedic contrast:

Take something trivial.

Film it as if it were life-or-death.

Example:

```text
A man tries to steal the last dumpling.

Film it like a martial-arts duel.

Extreme close-ups.

Dramatic eye contact.

Slow push-in.

Hand movements.

Suspenseful music.

Long silence.

He grabs the dumpling.

His mother says:

"That's mine."

He immediately puts it back.
```

---

# 43. THE "TOO CASUAL" PRINCIPLE

Take something extremely serious.

Treat it casually.

Example:

```text
A building is collapsing.

Everyone is running.

One character calmly finishes his noodles.

He looks up.

"...Can we move?"

```

Use contrast deliberately.

---

# 45. NEVER USE RANDOM CHAOS AS A SUBSTITUTE FOR WRITING

Bad:

```text
Everything explodes.
People fly everywhere.
Cars crash.
Someone screams.
```

Good:

```text
Character accidentally knocks over a cup.

Cup spills onto electrical socket.

Power goes out.

Automatic door locks.

Everyone becomes trapped.

Someone tries to force the door.

Door opens.

Everyone falls forward.

The original cup rolls back into frame.

```

The comedy comes from causal escalation.

---

# 46. COMEDIC CAUSALITY

Build chains:

```text
A causes B
B causes C
C causes D
D creates unexpected E
```

Example:

```text
Character sneezes.
↓
Drops phone.
↓
Phone lands on remote control.
↓
TV turns on.
↓
TV reveals secret.
↓
Everyone misunderstands secret.
↓
Fight begins.
```

This feels much more satisfying than random events.

---

# 47. THE "ONE SMALL MISTAKE" ENGINE

Start with a tiny mistake.

Then follow its consequences.

```text
Tiny mistake
↓
Attempt to hide it
↓
Cover-up creates second problem
↓
Second problem requires another lie
↓
Lie creates larger disaster
↓
Character loses control
↓
Unexpected payoff
```

This is one of the strongest comedy structures.

---

# 48. SOCIAL EMBARRASSMENT ENGINE

Another useful structure:

```text
Character wants to appear impressive.
↓
Something goes slightly wrong.
↓
Character hides it.
↓
Others notice.
↓
Character becomes more desperate.
↓
The attempt to hide it creates a bigger embarrassment.
```

---

# 49. COMEDY THROUGH COMMITMENT

Once a character makes a ridiculous decision, do not immediately abandon it.

Let them commit harder.

Example:

```text
He accidentally pretends to know martial arts.

Someone challenges him.

He could admit the truth.

Instead:

"Of course."

He prepares.

He has no idea what to do.

He continues pretending.

The situation becomes increasingly impossible.
```

The character's refusal to admit reality drives the comedy.

---

# 50. CALLBACK SYSTEM

Track potentially reusable elements:

```text
OBJECTS
PHRASES
GESTURES
CHARACTER TRAITS
MISTAKES
VISUAL DETAILS
RUNNING GAGS
```

When possible, bring them back later.

A callback should ideally:

* escalate
* reverse meaning
* surprise
* complete a setup

---

# 51. RUNNING GAGS

A recurring joke should evolve.

BAD:

```text
Same joke repeated 5 times.
```

BETTER:

```text
Occurrence 1:
Funny.

Occurrence 2:
Audience recognizes pattern.

Occurrence 3:
Expectation.

Occurrence 4:
Subversion.

Occurrence 5:
Final payoff.
```

---

# 55. ACTION COMEDY

Action should remain visually impressive.

The joke should come from:

* timing
* character behavior
* unexpected failure
* contrast
* props
* reversal

Do not make action intentionally ugly.

Make it look genuinely cinematic.

Then make the outcome ridiculous.

---

# 56. MARTIAL-ARTS COMEDY

When writing action:

```text
SERIOUS CHOREOGRAPHY
+
RIDICULOUS CHARACTER INTENT
```

Example:

A character performs an extremely elegant martial-arts sequence...

...only because he is trying to reach a mosquito.

The choreography is serious.

The objective is stupid.

That contrast is the joke.

---

# 57. EPICITY VS STUPIDITY

A recurring high-value formula:

```text
EPIC PRESENTATION
+
TRIVIAL OBJECTIVE
```

Examples:

* epic fight over the last dumpling
* dramatic chase for a slipper
* slow-motion battle over a remote control
* heroic sacrifice to save a cup of tea
* terrifying villain afraid of a cockroach

---

# 58. SURPRISE REVEAL ENGINE

Before every major reveal ask:

```text
What does the audience currently believe?

What would they expect next?

What is the most surprising result that still makes sense?
```

The best twist is not random.

It should make the audience think:

> "I didn't expect that, but it makes perfect sense."

---

# 59. MULTI-LAYERED JOKES

A strong scene can contain several jokes simultaneously.

Example:

Layer 1:
Character slips.

Layer 2:
He pretends he meant to do it.

Layer 3:
Another character believes him.

Layer 4:
The audience sees he accidentally activated something.

Layer 5:
That causes the main plot problem.

Layer 6:
The original slip becomes important later.

This creates density.
