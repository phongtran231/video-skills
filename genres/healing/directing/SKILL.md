# Module: Directing (camera, framing, motion)

- Shots are long, 8-10 seconds, and few; a scene may be one shot held from start to end.
- WHERE THE CAMERA STANDS: very far, like a painter at the edge of the field or square. About two of every three shots are EXTREME LONG SHOTS: the whole house, stone cottage or village in the frame, roof to garden or street, with the hills, fields, valley or sky behind it, seen from across the courtyard, the road or the hillside; the person a tiny figure on the terrace, balcony or stone steps, about a tenth of the frame's height or less. The place does the telling: light, leaves, mist, smoke, the season.
- The other wide shots are LONG SHOTS: the whole terrace, courtyard or room with the person full-length and small in it (a fifth of the frame's height or less), never cut at the knees or the waist.
- Some shots are of the place alone or with the people only as a small part of it (flower boxes on a stone sill with the person far behind them, wind moving through the trees while they walk away).
- Even a gesture is filmed from far: the person tending flowers or lighting a lantern is seen from across the courtyard, the whole architecture around them; the viewer reads the gesture from their whole body, never from fingers or face. Count it: in a film of 6 shots, at least 4 are wide or extreme wide, at most 1 is medium (medium-wide counts as medium) and at most 1 is a close-up; in a film of 9 shots, at least 6 wide. A beat that "needs" a close look gets the one close-up, or stays wide.
- Never two shots in a row that are not wide: after a medium or a close-up, the camera goes back out wide.
- Far shots in a row are right in this genre: the general rule of changing the shot size between consecutive shots does not apply; change where the camera stands instead (across the courtyard, from the cobblestone lane, from the hillside, from inside through an open doorway, stone arch or window).
- Rewriting existing shots (a reshoot): change their framing to this, whatever they were, and their rules line with it; keep only their story beat.
- A far shot's beats are a few large, slow movements seen whole (she sits, she rises, walks to the window, steps out to the terrace), never a small gesture the model would come closer to show.
- A scene opens wide (where we are, the season, the hour) and closes wide (the person small in the place, the feeling left in the landscape).
- The camera: static in a far shot (a push-in brings it close: never in a far shot); at most a very slow drift sideways or a slow pull-back that lets the place grow even bigger around the person. Never a whip, a crane swoop, a handheld shake or two moves.
- The beats: one small gesture at a time, given its full time (the old man strikes a match and lights the candle, 0-5s; the child watches the flame, 5-10s), and the place keeps living around it.
- motion: small continuous movement, calm energy: breeze in hair, cloth and leaves, flickering flame, drifting steam or smoke, slow blinks, a small shift of weight; "gentle, unhurried, never frozen, never fast".
