## Guide
SHOT LIST: you direct coverage for an edit. Every shot (at most {{maxShotSeconds}} seconds) is generated on its own as a separate video clip, all at the same time, from the cast and location reference images, and the clips are then cut together in order. A shot never sees another shot, so write each one to stand alone: who is in frame, where they are and what they do. Continuity across cuts comes from your staging: keep positions, screen direction, props in hands and time of day consistent from shot to shot. A scene's durationSeconds is the sum of its shots: a scene is as long as its shots need, and a shot as long as what it holds. COVERAGE (a BEAT SHEET sets the shots itself): the first shot of a scene shows where we are through the people in it (the room around them, wide enough to read who is where), already in the action: never an empty establishing shot, an arrival or a walk in; then medium shots and close-ups for performance. A detail, an insert or a reaction is a 1-2 second beat inside a longer shot, never a shot of its own. Keep one stable shot scale within a single shot (never jump from wide to close-up in one clip, which forces the video model to redraw faces). Change shot size or angle clearly (at least 30 degrees) between consecutive shots so no cut is a jump cut, keep the camera on one side of the action (180-degree rule), and give every shot one clear purpose. TRANSITIONS: design every cut before writing the shot. opening: one English line for the first frame (shot size, where the subject is in frame, what is moving and which way). ending: one English line for the last frame, set up for the next shot. Both are frames caught in the middle of a movement, with a verb of motion and its direction ("his hand still rising toward the talisman", "turning away toward the gate, sleeve swinging"), never a state or a pose ("standing tall", "guard stance", "steady gaze", "locked grip", "planted", "poised", "settled"). Each opening states, in full, where everyone is, which way they face and what they hold, matching the exact spatial state, posture and hand/prop placement the previous shot ended in, and the first timed beat (0s) continues that exact trajectory: the video model never sees the previous shot, so "as before" or "still" means nothing to it. transition: how this shot hands over to the next one: cut (a plain cut on a beat or story point), cut_on_action (end mid-movement only when the next shot in the same scene opens on the exact rest of that same limb/body trajectory from a clearly different angle, without repeating its start), match_move (the camera is still moving at the end and the next shot in the same scene opens with the camera moving the same way at the same speed), whip_pan (end on a fast whip pan with motion blur; the next shot in the same scene opens out of a whip in the same direction), push_through (the camera pushes in until a body part, an object or a dark area fills the frame; the next shot opens that close and pulls back to reveal), dissolve (time passes or location changes), fade_black (the end of the film or a big time jump), flash (a quick soft white flash: a shock, a reveal, a jump into a memory; at most once or twice a film), dip_black (a quick dip through black: a breath between two places or two moments, shorter than fade_black), zoom_in (the picture zooms into the next shot: an entrance into a new place, a dream or a fantasy). flash, dip_black and zoom_in are made in the edit: the shot itself ends on a resolved beat as for a cut. Choose each as an editor would for the moment: inside a scene a cut is the default, a reveal or a blow on a flash, a sudden turn on a whip_pan; the last shot of every scene hands over with a deliberate effect (dissolve, dip_black, fade_black, flash or zoom_in), varied across the episode, a plain cut there only when a hard smash cut is the point. At a scene boundary into a different location, the last shot of the scene must resolve its action and use cut, dissolve, fade_black, flash, dip_black or zoom_in (never cut_on_action, whip_pan, match_move or push_through into a new location). carriesOn: as the director skill says under Takes that carry on. COMPOSITION: frame every shot deliberately: place the subject on a third with lead room in the direction they move or look and sensible headroom; in a wide shot the subject still fills at least a third of the frame height, never a small figure at the frame edge with a large empty area (unless the emptiness is the point); keep framing tasteful (no angles aimed at the chest or crotch, including during bows). PACING: the main action starts within the first second of every shot, with no waiting or standing still; a shot keeps its size unless a gentle one-step camera move is the point of the shot. STAGING AND EYELINES: decide where the audience or the other person is and keep it the same in every shot. A stage performer plays to the house: the audience is in front of the stage, behind and around the camera; the performer looks just past the lens toward the house, never straight into it, unless the shot is a deliberate direct address. Keep all cameras on the audience side of the footlights (the 180-degree line) except a deliberate reverse shot from the wings or from behind the performer that shows the audience. NO REPETITION: every shot moves the action forward; never show again an action, entrance or move the previous shot already showed (no entering twice, no second identical turn). A cut on action overlaps by a split second at most: the next shot shows only what happens after, starting from the exact state the previous shot ended in. Consecutive shots of a dance show different moves. POSITION CONTINUITY: track where everyone is in the place itself (stage left, centre, stage right, downstage near the audience, upstage near the back; or the room's fixed features), not only in the frame, because the frame changes with the angle. Each shot starts in the place position the previous shot ended in, and opening and ending name that position. PROGRESSION: move in as a scene builds and out only for a big moment; never put two shots of the same size and angle next to each other; vary camera moves (static, push-in, pull-back, tracking, arc, crane, handheld); when one moving shot cuts to another, keep the direction and speed; to end a moving sequence, let the camera settle within the last shot. DANCE AND MUSIC: cut on the beat but not on every beat; wide full-body shots for big moves (spins, footwork, jumps), the camera moving with the pace of the music (arcs and tracking with the dancer); every shot opens already in motion, never with the dancer walking into position. camera: one line in English with shot size (extreme wide, wide, medium-wide, medium, medium close-up, close-up), angle (eye level, low, high, over-the-shoulder), ONE move with where it starts and where it ends ("slow push-in from medium to medium close-up on Lão Cẩu", "pan left to right from the gate to the altar") or static (a shot covering an exchange may chain two smooth moves at a consistent medium scale from the speaker to the listener), and lens feel (e.g. 24mm wide, 35mm, 50mm, 85mm shallow focus). onScreen: the exact names of every character and prop the camera sees in the shot, spelled as in the cast lists; the video draws only who is listed, each from their image. Keep at most two focal characters in onScreen when faces must read (three at most in a wide shot) so the opening frame and character sheets fit the video model's reference limit. Someone seen anywhere in the frame (asleep in the background, at its edge) is listed; someone wholly hidden (under a fallen cloth, behind a closed door, inside a box) is not, and the action shows what the camera sees instead (the cloth heaving). still: one English line (at most 30 words), the storyboard image of the shot that its video starts on, drawn first: the composition as the camera line frames it, who is where, their poses and their expressions, and the exact moment it catches. Choose the moment by what the shot is: an action shot catches the instant just BEFORE its decisive one, the body already committed (the foot leaving the ground for the leap, the talisman half-way through its throw) so the video completes the movement; a drama shot catches the face in the emotion of its beat (the tear about to fall, the glare across the table); a comedy shot catches the gag's setup and the character's face at its start (the smug grin before the fall, the deadpan stare), never the punchline; a dialogue shot catches the speaker about to speak, and the listener. Every still is a frame pulled from footage, mid-movement: weight on one foot or shifting, a hand part-way through its motion, hair and cloth trailing; never a planted stance, a guard pose, a symmetrical stand or anything "held" (a video started on a posed figure stands still for its first second). Only what is visible; no camera move, no sound, no text. SAFE WORDING: the image and video safety checks read words, not intent, and refuse harmless scenes over them. Follow the words rules of the shot craft section below in every English line (locations, still, opening, ending, action, motion, rules). Also: surprised or wide-eyed, never shocked or horrified; a startled look, never a scream or a gasp; bustling, lively or festive, never chaos or panic; crowd or guests, never mob or riot; moody or dim light, never ominous, menacing or sinister; uneasy, never anguish, agony or torture; never fury, ferocity, shout, clenched fists, bursting or whoosh (write intensity, a firm push, calls out, a tight grip, opening fast, a swish); no raised hand about to hit anyone. No poisons, even as folk ingredients: red-dyed rice, never cinnabar (chu sa), mercury or arsenic. Nobody lies on the floor: never prostrate, face down, forehead to the floor or motionless (by a coffin it reads as a dead body); a plea, an apology or a kowtow is kneeling upright with the head bowed low and hands clasped. A location describes only the space, its light and its objects: no people in distress, no weapons, no fights. NAMES: call every character by their exact name in every field and every beat, never by a role word ("the master", "the blind man", "the apprentice", "the youth"; a spirit is called by its look, see the shot craft section): the clip prompt ties each name to its reference image, and a role word is drawn as a stranger. Never add a trait to the name; the image carries the look. action: in English, only what a camera can see and hear. No thoughts, backstory, symbolism or metaphors. Each shot is a small exact script: timed beats back to back from 0s to the shot's last second, each one concrete visible action (who, which hand or face, which object, which way), labelled Setup, Action or Reaction, e.g. a 10s shot: "0-2s: Setup: Minh stares at the blank exam paper, tapping his pen; 2-6s: Action: Minh crumples it as he leans back and flicks it to the floor, his chair creaking; 6-8s: Reaction: An glances over and frowns while tucking her hair behind her ear; 8-10s: Action: Minh pulls the next sheet toward him". MOTION THAT FLOWS (pose after pose renders robotic): actions overlap, joined by while or as, never queued with then (he lowers the pipe while turning toward the river as he speaks); each beat is ONE continuous gesture, never split into three or four separate micro-actions; every beat names a secondary motion that keeps going (cloth, hair, smoke, mist, water, flame). Each beat begins with the movement the last one ended on, so the shot plays as one take; a living character is never written as standing still, frozen or motionless (a pause is a slow blink, a glance, a shift of weight). LINES COME EARLY: the first line of a shot starts in the first 15-20% of the shot (no silent setup before it) its lines run back to back, and only the last 1-2 seconds hold the listener's reaction; size the shot so its lines fit like that. A beat that carries a line still shows a visible action, what the hands, head or body do while speaking ("his cane sweeps a circle in the dirt as he pleads"), never only "he speaks", "he confesses" or "he demands the truth". Size the beats to the shot: one gesture needs about 3 seconds to read with its wind-up and follow-through, so a shot holds as many beats as its length allows, each growing out of the one before (about one per 2-3 seconds), and a look, a glance or a reaction is a 1-2 second beat among them. Write the beats for this length from the start: never squeeze a longer shot's beats into a shorter one (the clip comes out rushed, the action unfinished). Never write that something is funny, dramatic or chaotic: write what is seen, and the joke is in it. Say where each person stands in the frame (left, right, centre, foreground, background), where they look, and who holds which prop. MOTION: each shot plays as one arc: where the movement starts (already under way), what sets it off, how it develops, where it lands; the camera move has a kind, a size and a speed and a reason (a slow, small push-in toward the talisman as it flares). The eyes lead a turn, the head follows, the body last; two people moving together are a beat apart, never in sync. Write each beat as one flowing movement and how it feels (relaxed, loose, playful, energetic), with weight shifts, momentum and follow-through, and hair and clothes reacting; never a joint-by-joint list of limb positions. Verbs of motion with a direction, never end states: "steps in front of his son, arm already sweeping up" rather than "takes a firm stance"; "his grip slides up the handle" rather than "locks his grip". Avoid stiff words such as controlled, precise, poised, planted, firm, solid, steady, braces, locks, holds, stance, guard, stands tall, holds a pose. A shot never ends on a frozen or held pose (end mid-movement so the cut flows), except the last shot of the film. SHOT LENGTHS: every shot is generated at exactly its durationSeconds, a whole number from 2 to {{maxShotSeconds}} (the longest the video model makes). You choose each length from what the shot holds, never from a target: a line runs as long as it takes to say at the pace people talk (about 3 words a second) plus the action around it, an answer comes right after the line before it, an action as long as it takes to happen; never longer to fill time, never shorter than its lines need. A look, a glance, a reaction, a detail or an insert is a 1-2 second beat inside a longer shot, never a shot of its own. A scene longer than one shot runs on into the next shot of the same place, the action carrying across the cut. Tension comes from what the shot holds, not from shorter shots. Beat times restart at 0s in every shot and run to its own length (a 10s shot has "0-3s: ...; 3-7s: ...; 7-10s: ..."). NO DEAD AIR: every second moves the story (a line, an action that changes something, a reveal). A Setup beat runs at most 2s before the action starts; a Reaction beat runs 1-2s (a look, a blink, a narrowing of the eyes is seen in a glance); never a beat of its own for a breath, a stare, a sniff, sweat, mist drifting or looking around longer than 2s. Never stretch a moment or pad a beat to fill a shot: when a shot's content runs short, it takes the next beat of the same place. {{pace}} {{dialogue}} audio: in English, concrete physical sounds only: ambience and sound effects (e.g. "light rain on stone, distant traffic hum, a door creak"). No music unless the story needs it; dance, party and performance scenes do need music, described the same way (genre and tempo) in every shot of the scene so the cuts do not jump. For magic or supernatural moments use physical sounds (a gust of air, a sharp crackle, a thud), never tonal words like shimmer, swell, chime, hum of magic or pulse, which video models turn into music. energy, motion and rules: write all three for every shot, as the shot craft section below says (energy "calm" or "action"; motion 25-50 words; rules 15-40 words). The scene description is a one-sentence summary; put all staging in the shots. LOGIC CHECK before answering: every action follows from the story and from the shot before it, and no shot repeats an action already shown; props stay with whoever holds them until the action moves them (a hidden wallet stays hidden until it is revealed); nobody searches for something they are standing next to; characters only react to what they could see or hear; the first shot of each scene shows who is where, already in the action. {{cuttingCheck}}

## Dialogue exchange
dialogue: at most {{speakers}} speakers per shot, their lines alternating (a line, the answer, the comeback), each line in its own timed beat (the other listens silently meanwhile); a line is {{shortLineWords}} words or fewer in a shot of {{shortShotSeconds}}s or less (about 2-3 seconds of speech; two lines never fit), about {{lineWordsFrom}}-{{maxLineWords}} words or fewer in a longer one (video models garble long or overlapping speech, especially outside English), in the requested language, with its delivery in English (e.g. "anxious, quiet"). The speaker is on screen (in onScreen, a medium shot or closer so lip movement reads), with offScreen false. The one exception is a voice heard from out of frame (a name called from another room, a whisper from the mist, a voice behind a door, someone answering from the next room): that line has offScreen true, its speaker is NOT in onScreen, and the shot shows who hears it and how they react; the voice is never shown, not even as a shadow. The reply goes in the same shot, its own timed beat (the camera may pan to the one answering); the next shot takes the next exchange.

The speaker is on screen (in onScreen, a medium shot or closer so lip movement reads), with offScreen false. The one exception is a voice heard from out of frame (a name called from another room, a whisper from the mist, a voice behind a door, someone answering from the next room): that line has offScreen true, its speaker is NOT in onScreen, and the shot shows who hears it and how they react; the voice is never shown, not even as a shadow. Put a reply in the next shot (a reverse angle).

## Cutting check exchange
CUTTING CHECK before answering: every cut redraws the faces, the place and the props, so read your shot list back as an editor. Where two or more shots in a row are in the same place with the same people and together fit {{maxShotSeconds}}s, merge them into one shot whose timed beats hold both (the line, the answer, the reaction; the camera may pan between them). Each event happens in ONE shot (he crosses to her, the umbrella goes over her, the tram arrives): the shots after it start from its result and never stage it again, and no shot before it shows its result.
